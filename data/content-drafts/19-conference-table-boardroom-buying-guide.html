Size: seats first, then the room

Plan roughly two feet (about 24 inches) of table edge per person as a minimum, with 30 inches giving a more comfortable, spacious feel. Then check it against the room: a common rule is to leave about three feet of clearance on every side for chairs and walking room, so subtract roughly eight to ten feet from the room length to find the longest table that fits. Whichever number is smaller, seats or room, sets your size.

Shape: match it to how you meet

  • Rectangular is the default for formal boardrooms and presentations, with a clear head of the table.
  • Boat-shaped tapers the ends so everyone can see each other and the screen, which is why it is a popular boardroom choice; a commercial boat-shape like the Global Zira boat-shaped table is a typical example.
  • Round or square suits small, equal, collaborative meetings of four to six, with no head of the table.
  • Modular or flip-top tables reconfigure or fold and nest, which suits training and multi-purpose rooms; flip-top training tables are built exactly for that.

Power, data, and cable management

The most common boardroom regret is a beautiful table no one can plug into. Specify integrated power and data modules in the tabletop, with grommets and a cable pathway down to the floor, so a laptop, a screen, and a video call all connect without trailing cords. If the room is used for video conferencing, plan the AV and camera sightlines at the same time as the table, because the table shape and the camera position have to agree.

Boardroom vs conference room

The terms overlap, but the use differs. A boardroom is usually an intimate, fixed room for senior leadership and formal decisions, so it leans toward one statement table and executive seating. A conference room is more flexible and often larger, used for varied meetings and presentations, so it benefits from modular tables and stackable or lighter seating that reconfigures. Buy the table for the way the room is actually used, not the label on the door.

Material and the rest

For commercial use, a quality laminate top is the workhorse: durable, easy to clean, and available in finishes that match your space, while veneer and solid surfaces read more premium for a flagship boardroom. In every case, buy commercial-grade so the edge banding and base survive years of use, and match the meeting seating to the table so the room reads as one. Frequently Asked Questions

How do you size a conference table for a room? Allow about two feet of table edge per person, then make sure the table leaves roughly three feet of clearance on all sides for chairs and walking room, which usually means subtracting eight to ten feet from the room length. Use whichever is smaller, the seat count or the room fit, as your size. For a 12-foot table you generally want a room about 16 by 18 feet.

How many people can sit at a conference table? As a rule of thumb, allow two feet per person, so a 6-foot table seats about 4 to 6, an 8-foot table about 6 to 8, a 10-foot about 8 to 10, and a 12-foot about 10 to 12, with a couple more if you seat the ends. Boat and rectangular shapes seat the most for their length; round tables seat fewer but suit small equal meetings.

What is the difference between a boardroom and a conference room? A boardroom is typically an intimate, fixed room for senior leadership and formal decisions, often with one statement table and executive chairs. A conference room is usually more flexible and can hold more people in varied layouts, so it suits modular tables and lighter, reconfigurable seating. Specify the furniture for how the room is used rather than its name.

What shape of conference table is best? Rectangular suits formal boardrooms with a clear head of table; boat-shaped improves sightlines to people and screens; round or square suits small, equal, collaborative meetings; and modular or flip-top tables suit training and multi-purpose rooms. Choose the shape by your meeting style and the room, then size it to seats and clearance.

Does a conference table need built-in power? For almost any modern meeting, yes. Integrated power and data modules with grommets and a cable path to the floor let people connect laptops, screens, and video calls without trailing cords, which is the difference between a table that works and one that frustrates every meeting. Plan it with the AV if the room is used for video conferencing.
