The three options for an office

There are really three choices, and most offices land on a mix rather than all of one.

Sit-stand vs fixed-height vs fixed-standing desks for an office
Desk type What it does Cost per desk Best for
Sit-stand (height-adjustable) Moves between sitting and standing at the touch of a button Highest Full-day desk roles; shared and hot-desks; anyone who benefits from movement
Fixed-height seated A traditional desk at one seated height Lowest Light-use, occasional, or touchdown desks; tight budgets
Fixed standing A standing-height desk that does not lower Low to moderate Reception, labs, collaboration counters where people stand anyway

Why sit-stand is usually the better default for full-time desks

For any desk where someone works most of the day, the ability to alternate between sitting and standing is the advantage. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety describes a sit-stand desk as one that lets a worker change posture through the day, and the value is the movement and posture change itself rather than standing for hours straight. A fixed-height desk locks one posture in place; a sit-stand desk lets each person find what works and change it. For full-time desk roles, that flexibility is why sit-stand is the sensible default, and it also future-proofs the desk for whoever sits there next.

The benefit comes from a sensible rhythm of alternating sitting and standing rather than from standing all day; our ergonomics guidance covers how to pace that and how seating fits in. Standing for seven hours straight is no better than sitting for seven, so the goal is variety, not maximum standing time.

When fixed-height desks still make sense

Sit-stand is not automatically right for every desk in the building. Fixed-height desks remain the practical choice in several spots:

  • Light-use and touchdown desks that people use for short stretches, not full days.
  • Tight budgets where the priority is seating every person commercial-grade first, then upgrading desks over time.
  • Specialised stations built around fixed equipment or a set worksurface height.
  • Reception and counters where a fixed standing height suits the task better than adjustability.

A common, cost-smart pattern is a mix: sit-stand at full-time workstations, fixed-height at light-use and touchdown spots. You get the movement where it matters and spend the budget where it counts.

How to decide for your office

Work it backward from how each desk is used. Map which roles sit all day (strong candidates for sit-stand), which desks are shared or hot-desked (sit-stand pays off because it fits everyone who uses it), which are light-use (fixed is fine), and what the budget allows this cycle. The answer almost always comes out of the layout rather than a blanket rule, which is why a planned floor plan makes the sit-stand-versus-fixed call for you, desk by desk. Frequently Asked Questions

Should an office choose sit-stand or fixed-height desks? For full-time desk roles and any shared or hot-desk, sit-stand is usually the better default because it lets each person alternate posture and fits whoever uses the desk next. Fixed-height desks still make sense for light-use, touchdown, and specialised stations, and for stretching a tight budget. Many offices use a mix, which is often the smartest spend.

Do you need to give everyone a sit-stand desk? No. The highest return is on desks where someone works most of the day and on shared desks used by different people. Light-use and touchdown spots can stay fixed-height. A mixed deployment, sit-stand where it earns its cost and fixed elsewhere, is usually the most cost-effective approach for a whole office.

Are sit-stand desks worth it for a business? For full-day workstations, generally yes: the movement and posture flexibility benefit the people using them, and commercial sit-stand desks last for years across a fleet. The benefit comes from alternating sitting and standing, not from standing all day, so pair the desks with a sensible rhythm. For light-use desks the extra cost is harder to justify.

What is the difference between a sit-stand desk and a fixed standing desk? A sit-stand, or height-adjustable, desk moves between sitting and standing heights, so one desk suits both postures and a range of people. A fixed standing desk stays at a standing height and does not lower. Fixed standing suits spots where people stand anyway, like reception or a collaboration counter, while sit-stand suits a full-time workstation.

Is standing all day at a desk better than sitting? No. Standing for long unbroken stretches brings its own strain, just as sitting all day does. The point of a sit-stand desk is to change posture regularly rather than to replace sitting with standing. For specific back or joint conditions, check with a clinician on what posture pattern suits you.

The bottom line

Sit-stand versus fixed is a deployment decision, not a one-size rule. Put sit-stand desks where people work all day and where desks are shared, use fixed-height desks for light-use and specialised spots, and let the layout decide the rest.
