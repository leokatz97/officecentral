Global Furniture Group (and Offices to Go)

Founded in Canada in 1966 and headquartered in the Toronto area, Global Furniture Group is one of the largest business-furniture manufacturers in the world and the workhorse of the Canadian market. Offices to Go is Global's value-focused line, popular for quick-ship task seating and casegoods. For a buyer who wants one Canadian maker that can furnish nearly an entire office, Global is usually the starting point, which is why it carries the deepest representation in our own catalogue.

Teknion

Teknion, headquartered in Toronto, is the name to know for architectural office systems: benching, panel systems, demountable walls, and the infrastructure of a large, planned fit-out. Where Global is broadest, Teknion is strongest at the systems end, the workstation runs and space-dividing architecture that a growing company or a campus floor plate needs. It competes directly with the US systems makers on contract projects.

Keilhauer

Also based in Toronto, Keilhauer is a design-led seating and lounge manufacturer whose work shows up in contract and hospitality-leaning workplaces. If the brief is a boardroom, a reception, or collaborative lounge zones where the furniture is part of the design story, Keilhauer is a Canadian maker that competes on aesthetics and comfort rather than price.

ergoCentric

"Canadian company" is not the same as "Made in Canada"

This is the distinction that most guides skip, and it is the one that protects you in a procurement review. A manufacturer can be a Canadian-owned company headquartered in Toronto and still build a given product line, or source its components, outside Canada. The reverse is also true: a globally owned brand may assemble certain products in a Canadian plant. Corporate ownership and country of manufacture are two different facts, and a serious buyer keeps them separate.

The legal bar is specific. Under the Competition Bureau's guidance, a product can be labelled "Made in Canada" only when the last substantial transformation happened in Canada, at least 51 percent of the total direct production or manufacturing costs were incurred in Canada, and the claim carries a qualifying statement such as "Made in Canada with imported parts" where that applies. "Product of Canada" is a stricter test still, requiring essentially all significant costs to be Canadian. Because those thresholds are enforced and vary line by line, we do not stamp a blanket maple leaf across a catalogue. When Canadian content is part of your policy, we confirm the manufacturing origin of a specific line in writing rather than relying on a brand's national reputation.

This is live in 2026, not theoretical. Buy-Canadian preferences have moved into more procurement policies, and trade pressure has pushed some makers to shift work across the border: industry reporting in 2026 noted Prepac, a ready-to-assemble furniture manufacturer, preparing to move production from Canada to North Carolina, a reminder that "where it is made" can change even when the brand name does not. The takeaway for a buyer is simple. Use corporate origin to build your shortlist, then verify manufacturing origin per line before you write it into a tender or a sustainability claim.
