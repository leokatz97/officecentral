## FULL DRAFT (body HTML-ready)

**When employees say "hot-desking — hell no," they're not rejecting the idea. They're rejecting how it was done to them.** It's the single most-upvoted reaction in every office forum: a company went hybrid, pulled out the assigned desks, and replaced them with a grab-a-spot free-for-all — no storage, no way to set up your station, a different cold chair every morning. The concept didn't fail. The execution did, and almost all of it was a furniture and layout decision that nobody made on purpose.

This guide is the furniture side of the hybrid-desk question — the part the software vendors and definition pages skip. What hot-desking actually is, the three ways to allocate desks (and who each suits), how many desks a hybrid team genuinely needs, why hot-desking fails and the specific furniture that fixes each failure, and how to choose a model that your team won't revolt against. It's for the Ontario office or facility manager deciding what to do with the desks now that the team is in two or three days a week.

### What hot-desking is — and why teams resist it

Hot-desking (also called desk-sharing or hoteling) is a system where desks aren't assigned — people use whatever's available, often booking a spot for the day. It exists because hybrid schedules made assigned desks wasteful: if a team of 20 is never more than 12-in on a given day, eight desks sit empty every day, taking up space and rent.

The logic is sound. The resistance is real, and it's almost always about loss: nowhere to keep your things, no consistent setup, no sense of a place that's yours, and the hygiene of a shared surface. Every one of those is fixable — and the fix is furniture and policy, not abandoning the model.

Most successful hybrid offices use a blend: a core of shared desks, a few fixed desks for roles that need them, and activity-based zones (focus pods, collaboration tables, lounge) so people aren't stuck at one kind of seat all day.

### How many desks do you actually need?

The whole point of hot-desking is fewer desks than people — but how many fewer depends on your real in-office pattern, not a guess. The lever is the **desk-sharing ratio**: desks ÷ headcount.

- If your team averages **60% in-office** on a typical day, you might plan around **0.6–0.7 desks per person** plus a buffer for peak days.
- A heavily-remote team might run **0.5 or lower**; a mostly-in team shouldn't hot-desk much at all.
- Always plan for the **busiest realistic day**, not the average — the fastest way to kill hot-desking is for people to show up and find no desk.

⚠️ *(Leo: present these as planning heuristics, not standards; the real ratio comes out of the team's attendance data + a layout.)* The single most valuable thing you can do before buying anything is measure your actual attendance for a few weeks. That number sets the desk count, and the desk count sets the budget.

### The furniture that makes shared desks work

- **Benching systems** — efficient shared-desk runs designed for hot-desking, not repurposed assigned desks.
- **Height-adjustable (sit-stand) desks** — so each person resets the desk to themselves; the #1 fix for "it's never set up for me."
- **Personal lockers / day-storage** — the single biggest driver of acceptance.
- **Cleanable, commercial-grade surfaces** — for shared hygiene and daily-use durability.
- **Quiet backup** — phone booths + pods so shared space doesn't mean no privacy.
- **Worth-the-commute breakout** — lounge and collaboration settings that make the office a place people choose.

### Choosing your model — a decision guide

Start with one question: **how often is the team actually in?** Mostly-in → keep assigned desks. In 2–3 days a week → shared desks with the right ratio and the furniture above. Mixed focus and collaboration work → layer in activity-based zones. The model follows the attendance, and the furniture follows the model.

### Common Questions

**What is hot-desking?** A system where desks aren't assigned — people use whatever's available, often booking a spot for the day. It exists because hybrid schedules leave assigned desks empty and wasteful, so sharing them reclaims space and rent.

**Why does hot-desking fail?** Almost always because of execution, not the concept: no personal storage, desks that can't be set up to the user, hygiene concerns, and too few desks on busy days. Each of those is a furniture or policy fix — lockers, height-adjustable desks, cleanable surfaces, and the right desk ratio.

**How many desks does a hybrid team need?** Fewer than its headcount — set by the desk-sharing ratio (desks ÷ people) based on real in-office attendance, with a buffer for the busiest day. A team that's ~60% in might plan around 0.6–0.7 desks per person. Measure attendance before buying.

**What furniture makes hot-desking work?** Benching systems built for sharing, height-adjustable sit-stand desks, personal lockers or day-storage, cleanable commercial surfaces, and phone booths/pods for privacy. Lockers and adjustable desks fix the two biggest complaints.

**What's the difference between hot-desking and activity-based working?** Hot-desking shares the same kind of desk among people; activity-based working provides different settings for different tasks — focus, collaboration, social — and people move between them through the day. Many offices combine both.

**Should we hot-desk or keep assigned desks?** It depends on attendance. A mostly-in team should keep assigned desks; a team in two or three days a week is a strong candidate for shared desks with the right ratio and supporting furniture. The attendance pattern decides.
