## FULL DRAFT (body HTML-ready)

**The open-plan office solved one problem and created another: nobody can take a call or do focused work.** Open layouts went mainstream because they're flexible and fit more people in less space — both true. But the moment a team is actually in the room, the same openness that helps collaboration wrecks concentration. People book a six-person meeting room to take a one-person video call. Sensitive conversations happen in stairwells. The "we need more meeting rooms" complaint is usually really a "we have no quiet space" problem.

This guide is for the Ontario office or facility manager fixing that: why open plans lose focus work, the four ways to add quiet (and what each costs you in space and budget), how pods, phone booths, and meeting rooms differ, how many quiet spaces a team actually needs, and what to look for in an acoustic pod before you spend several thousand dollars on one. Quiet spaces are one of the highest-return additions to an open office — cheap relative to the productivity they buy back.

### Why open-plan offices lose focus work

Sound is the open plan's weak point. Conversation noise is especially disruptive because the brain can't tune out speech the way it tunes out steady background hum. The result is measurable: more interruptions, more errors on focused tasks, and the quiet ones on the team quietly disengaging. Adding quiet zones isn't a luxury — it's the correction that makes the open plan actually work for the focus and private-conversation parts of the job.

The pod's advantage over a built room is that it's furniture, not construction — it installs in a day and moves when your lease does. For a leased Ontario office, that's often the deciding factor.

### How many quiet spaces does your team need?

There's no rule that fits every office, but a workable planning starting point for a knowledge-work team: **roughly one phone-booth-style space per 8–12 people**, plus small meeting pods scaled to how often the team takes group calls. ⚠️ *(Leo: this is a planning heuristic, not a standard — frame as guidance, verify before presenting as fact.)* Teams that are heavily hybrid (lots of video calls) need more; heads-down teams need fewer but value them more. The right number comes out of the layout, which is exactly what a design layout is for.

### What to look for in an acoustic pod

Before spending several thousand dollars per unit, check:

- **Measured noise reduction** — look for a stated dB reduction, ideally ISO-tested (e.g. 25–30+ dB). "Soundproof" with no number is marketing.
- **Ventilation** — active airflow so it stays usable for more than a few minutes.
- **Power + connectivity** — outlets/USB and good lighting for real work and calls.
- **Footprint + door swing** — confirm it fits the spot *and* the circulation around it.
- **Movability + warranty** — can it relocate with your lease, and what's the commercial warranty?
- **Glass + finish** — visibility for safety/booking, finish that matches the space.

### Placing quiet spaces in your layout

Where the pods go matters as much as how many. Booths near high-traffic zones get used most; pods tucked into dead corners reclaim otherwise-wasted square footage. ### Common Questions

**How do you reduce noise in an open-plan office?** Layer it: acoustic panels and desk dividers to lower the overall noise floor, plus enclosed phone booths and pods for calls and private conversations. No single product fixes an open plan — the mix does.

**What's the difference between a phone booth and an office pod?** A phone booth is a one-person enclosure for calls and short focus work; a pod is a larger 2–6-person enclosure for huddles and hybrid meetings. Both install like furniture and move with your lease, unlike a built meeting room.

**How many phone booths does an office need?** A workable starting point is roughly one phone-booth-style space per 8–12 people, plus meeting pods scaled to how often the team takes group calls. Heavily hybrid teams need more. The right number comes out of your layout.

**Are office pods soundproof?** Good ones provide measured noise reduction — often 25–30+ dB on ISO-tested models — which is "very quiet," not literally soundproof. Always look for a stated dB figure rather than the word "soundproof" alone.

**Do acoustic pods need installation or construction?** No construction — pods and booths are furniture. They drop into place, usually install in a day, and can relocate when your lease changes, which is their main advantage over building a room.

**Can pods be moved if we relocate?** Yes — that's a key reason leased Ontario offices choose pods over built rooms. They disassemble and relocate with your team.
